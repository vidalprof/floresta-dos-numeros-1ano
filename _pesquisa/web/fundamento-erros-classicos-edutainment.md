The two researchers, from the University of Nottingham in England, recently decided to find out whether children could detect such subterfuge, and whether they benefited more from lessons that masquerade as games—or from games that make learning an end in itself.

The results were clear: The children who had played the intrinsically-rewarding game learned more math. Next, the researchers allowed another group of children to choose the option they preferred. The verdict here was even more definitive: The pupils spent seven times longer playing the intrinsic version of Zombie Division.

Many educational games fail to live up to their promise of effective, enjoyable learning; Habgood and Ainsworth suggest that’s because the games rely on an extrinsic reward structure, bestowing gold stars for good performance instead of making the incentives internal to the game. The key to creating a successful educational game, the authors conclude, is what they call “intrinsic integration”: ensuring that the mechanics of the game mesh tightly with the content the game is trying to teach. It’s a lesson that applies to offline education, too: Make the vegetables themselves taste good, and you won’t have to bribe kids with chocolate.

Why Gamification Usually Fails | Behavioral Strategy

: Most gamification fails because it focuses on superficial game elements (points, badges, leaderboards) rather than understanding the underlying behaviors and motivations. Success requires aligning game mechanics with intrinsic motivation and actual user needs.

Common pattern: many gamification projects fail to meet their objectives. Here’s why.

1. The PBL Fallacy (Points, Badges, Leaderboards)

Extrinsic motivation crowds out intrinsic

- Users who enjoyed the activity now do it for points

- Leaderboards often demotivate lower-ranked users

Boundary Conditions (when mechanics are least likely to backfire)

: the behavior itself produces validated value for the user as well as the organization.

: Corporate Compliance Training Gamification

: Complete the compliance module in one session

Initial Engagement: High first-week participation

30-Day Retention: Steep drop after week two

90-Day Retention: Low ongoing participation

Behavior Change: No measurable change in on-the-job compliance behaviors

Game Mechanics: Points, badges, leaderboard

Reward System: Team recognition and small rewards

: Rewards competed with intrinsic motivation and didn’t address low relevance.

: Drop-off after the first module; negative comments in surveys.

: Time pressure, unclear value, lack of manager reinforcement.

Start with behavior fit before mechanics.

Use incentives only when they reinforce existing motivation.

Instead of: Layering points on mandatory content

We would: Redesign the content for relevance, then test a small opt-in pilot

Gamification fails when it’s applied as a bandage to poor design or used to manipulate rather than motivate. Success requires:

Sustainable design for long-term engagement

Constant iteration based on behavioral data

: If you’re adding games to make something tolerable, first ask why it’s intolerable. Fix that first.
