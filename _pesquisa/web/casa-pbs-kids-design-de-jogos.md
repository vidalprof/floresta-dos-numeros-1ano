## Children's Content | PBS

`https://www.pbs.org/standards/childrens-content/`

PBS believes that children are a special audience with exciting potential and possibilities for learning and growth. However, children are still developing critical reasoning skills that can make them more vulnerable than adults to misleading and harmful media messages and images. As such, PBS seeks to provide children with engaging and developmentally-appropriate content, including video, interactive experiences, educational games, and audio content, that nurtures each child’s total well-being. Content must address social, emotional, cognitive, physical, and language development and also focus on major curriculum areas, including literacy, math, science, engineering, technology, arts, and social studies.

Incorporate Developmentally-Appropriate Goals

All children’s content must have explicit developmentally-appropriate educational goals that are clearly incorporated into format design, character development, settings, and storylines. Dangerous, illegal, or otherwise anti-social behaviors that could impact a child’s sense of well-being, or that could be easily mimicked by a child, should be avoided (such as name-calling; bullying; rudeness and sarcasm; gratuitous physical or psychological violence; the use of hazardous household items such as razors, knives, or matches; the misuse of technology or social media; and the use of drugs, alcohol, or tobacco).

PBS provides producers with standards-aligned learning frameworks for core curriculum areas, developed with subject matter experts and child development advisors, to ensure content supports developmentally-appropriate skills and concepts. The production teams for children's content must include early childhood education advisors to ensure that all content goals are aligned with learning framework milestones. Producers should also make use of child development and early learning research and best practices throughout the production process.

PBS strives to distribute content that reflects and responds to the different environments and experiences in which children grow, live, learn, and play. The principle of inclusiveness requires producers to be sensitive and thoughtful when stories involve dimensions of identity. In addition, any insulting or demeaning stereotypes and derogatory epithets are not permitted.

## PBS KIDS Transmedia Suites Gaming Study | CCT

PBS KIDS Transmedia Suites Gaming Study | CCT

The CPB-PBS Ready To Learn initiative, funded by the U. S. Department of Education, brings engaging, high-quality media to young children who may be at risk for academic difficulties due to economic and social disadvantages. The initiative aims to deliver early mathematics and literacy resources on new and emerging digital platforms such as tablet computers, interactive whiteboards, and smartphones, as well as better-established technologies such as computers, video displays, and gaming consoles, and to create learning experiences that leverage the unique capabilities of these various technology platforms.

The 2012 PBS KIDS Transmedia Suites Gaming Study is an important part of the multiyear Ready To Learn (RTL) summative evaluation initiative by Education Development Center, Inc., (EDC) and SRI International (SRI) for the Corporation for Public Broadcasting (CPB) and the Public Broadcasting Service (PBS). The study explored student engagement in transmedia gaming by examining contexts of use and analyzing patterns of use during game play by children. Building on ongoing gaming research in the education community as well as within prior Ready To Learn studies, we investigated how children and educators made use of PBS KIDS transmedia designed to support learning across a range of subject areas, including mathematics, language and literacy, and science.

The study was designed to take advantage of the opportunity to observe children and educators working with PBS KIDS transmedia over time. Researchers made two visits to two selected demonstration station sites over a period of several weeks and collected observation and interview data during both visits. We visited a variety of institutional settings, including summer learning programs, a city park, and community-based organizations.

The study used qualitative data-collection methods including observations and interviews with two different target populations: educators and parents/caregivers. In addition, brief informal interviews with children were incorporated during observation periods.

Data collection took place in two phases in order to allow for a period of time to pass during which children and educators working with them would have time to become familiar with specific PBS KIDS transmedia properties and also to allow for the development of a comfort level with the technology.

PBS Kids Games and the Case for Educational Screen Time. For parents across the world, the question is no longer whether children will engage with screens, but how—and to what effect. Tablets and smartphones have become fixtures of modern childhood, prompting persistent anxiety about attention spans, learning outcomes, and developmental health. Amid this concern, a growing body of educators and

specialists is calling for a more nuanced conversation—one that distinguishes between passive consumption and purposeful engagement.

Within that discussion, PBS Kids Games occupies a distinctive position. Developed under the umbrella of public broadcasting’s long-standing educational mission, these games represent an alternative model of digital interaction—one designed not to distract, but to teach.

, early learning, and digital trust. Rather than offering promotional claims, it analyzes how design philosophy, content structure, and public accountability shape a platform many parents consider a safer digital space for children.

The Evolution of Children’s Media: From Television to Interactive Learning

Public Broadcasting’s Educational Mandate

has historically played a central role in educational media, particularly for early childhood audiences. Long before the rise of apps and touchscreens, PBS programming focused on literacy, numeracy, and social development through carefully designed television shows.

PBS Kids emerged as a dedicated brand to extend this mission to younger audiences. As digital platforms expanded, PBS adapted its educational approach to interactive formats, giving rise to PBS Kids Games—a collection of digital activities tied to learning objectives rather than entertainment metrics.

Why Interactive Media Changed the Equation

Unlike traditional television, interactive games require participation. Children are not merely watching; they are responding, choosing, experimenting, and problem-solving. Educational psychologists have long emphasized that such engagement supports deeper learning, particularly in early developmental stages.

The challenge has been ensuring that interactivity serves learning rather than distraction. This is where PBS Kids Games distinguishes itself from many commercial alternatives.

Character-Driven Learning as a Pedagogical Tool

Children’s familiarity with characters plays a significant role in engagement. Characters from PBS programs serve as anchors, providing continuity between television content and interactive play.

In the context of PBS Kids Games, characters are not mascots but instructional tools. They model problem-solving behavior, encourage persistence, and reinforce learning concepts without overt instruction.

Educational Foundations: What Research Suggests

Play-Based Learning and Cognitive Development

Decades of educational research support the idea that children learn best through play. Play-based learning encourages exploration, experimentation, and self-directed discovery—key components of cognitive growth.

PBS Kids Games are structured around these principles. Tasks are framed as challenges rather than tests, allowing children to make mistakes and try again without penalty. This low-stakes environment supports confidence and curiosity.

Many games focus on early literacy, including letter recognition, phonics, vocabulary building, and story comprehension. These skills are foundational for later academic success.

Mathematics and Problem-Solving Through Digital Play

Early math education is most effective when abstract concepts are grounded in real-world scenarios. Games that incorporate counting, sorting, and pattern recognition help children build intuitive understanding.

PBS Kids Games frequently present math challenges as part of everyday activities—measuring ingredients, organizing objects, or navigating spaces. This contextual approach aligns with best practices in early math instruction.

Beyond numbers, problem-solving games promote sequencing, prediction, and cause-and-effect reasoning. These skills extend beyond academics, contributing to broader cognitive flexibility.

Such design choices position PBS Kids Games not as isolated learning tools, but as part of a holistic developmental framework.

Health organizations often emphasize limiting screen time, particularly for young children. However, experts increasingly stress that content quality matters as much as duration.

Educational screen time differs fundamentally from passive viewing. When children interact with thoughtfully designed games, they practice skills actively rather than absorbing content passively.

PBS Kids Games are frequently cited as examples of “high-quality” screen time due to their educational intent and non-commercial structure.

Unlike many children’s apps, PBS Kids Games operate without advertising-driven incentives. There are no in-app purchases or algorithmic rewards designed to maximize engagement at the expense of well-being.

This distinction is critical in evaluating trustworthiness. Parents are not navigating hidden monetization strategies, which strengthens the platform’s credibility.

Public broadcasting operates under a different accountability model than private entertainment companies. Educational objectives, funding sources, and content standards are more transparent.

PBS Kids Games benefit from this structure. Parents and educators can more easily assess the platform’s intentions, reducing uncertainty about data use or behavioral manipulation.

Another defining feature is accessibility. Many PBS Kids Games are free to use, supporting educational equity across socioeconomic backgrounds.

In an era where quality educational tools are often locked behind paywalls, this accessibility reinforces the platform’s public-service orientation.

Comparing PBS Kids Games With Commercial Alternatives

Entertainment-First Versus Education-First Design

Commercial children’s games often prioritize visual stimulation and reward loops. While engaging, these designs can emphasize speed and repetition over comprehension.

PBS Kids Games, by contrast, prioritize pacing and understanding. Success is measured not by how quickly a child completes a task, but by whether they grasp the underlying concept.

Design elements such as muted colors, gentle audio cues, and clear instructions reduce cognitive overload. These choices support focus, particularly for younger users.

Such restraint reflects a pedagogical philosophy rather than a marketing one.

Learning How to Use Technology Responsibly

Beyond academic skills, children must learn how to interact with technology itself. Educational platforms can model healthy digital behaviors, including patience, reflection, and goal-oriented engagement.

PBS Kids Games implicitly teach these behaviors by avoiding endless scrolling or reward addiction. Games have clear endpoints, encouraging natural breaks.

As technology becomes inseparable from education and work, early exposure to responsible digital tools may shape long-term habits.

In this sense, PBS Kids Games contribute not only to immediate learning, but to digital literacy—a skill increasingly recognized as essential.

No Digital Tool Is a Substitute for Human Interaction

While PBS Kids Games offer educational value, experts emphasize that digital tools should complement, not replace, human interaction. Conversation, physical play, and real-world exploration remain central to development.

Parents and caregivers play a critical role in contextualizing screen-based learning, discussing content and extending lessons offline.

Not all games are suitable for all ages. Adult guidance helps ensure children engage with content aligned to their developmental stage.

Used thoughtfully, PBS Kids Games function best as part of a balanced learning environment.

Why PBS Kids Games Endure in a Crowded Digital Landscape

In a market saturated with children’s apps, longevity is often driven by aggressive monetization or viral appeal. PBS Kids Games endure for a different reason: institutional trust built over decades.

Their continued relevance suggests that educational integrity, when paired with thoughtful design, can compete successfully with more commercial offerings.

Conclusion: Screen Time Parents Can Evaluate With Confidence

The debate over children and screens is unlikely to disappear. As technology evolves, so too will concerns about its influence on young minds. Yet blanket rejection of digital tools overlooks the potential benefits of carefully designed educational media.

PBS Kids Games demonstrate that screen time can be purposeful, measured, and developmentally supportive. Grounded in public broadcasting’s educational mission, the platform offers a model for how digital learning tools can earn parental trust without sacrificing engagement.

For families navigating the complexities of modern childhood, the question may no longer be whether children use screens—but whether the screens they use are worthy of their attention.
