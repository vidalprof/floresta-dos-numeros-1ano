Mapping Learning-Game Mechanics and Motivation (LM-GM-SDT)

The mapping of learning onto gameplay is essential for informing the types of engagement and interaction we wish to design and formulate. And the need is for game mechanics to tap into motivation — how learners are encouraged or prompted to engage with the learning contents through the medium of games or game-like activities. As discussed in Chapter 3, SDT (Deci and Ryan, 2000) is often associated with basic psychological needs in games, emphasising autonomy, competence, and relatedness as aspects that can positively influence intrinsic motivation as well as autonomous motivation.

Building on the ‘game elements — autonomous motivation’ mapping, we can explore how these aspects can be linked to how learning is facilitated through gameplay. Proulx et al. (2016) suggest that a game design model can be combined with motivational theory. They propose a framework, based on the LM-GM model, that aims to articulate and analyse the motivational potential of games by analysing autonomous motivation, and map this against the ludic and pedagogical constructs (see Figure 4.4). The LM-GM classification based on Bloom’s taxonomy (Amab et al., 2015) is mapped against the self-determination continuum (Ryan and Deci, 2009), emphasising autonomous motivation that is often linked to positive impact on academic achievement (Taylor et al., 2014) and engagement (Lee, 2005). In the case of game-based learning, the intended play-leam activities ‘should be in coherence with four requirements of the player activity: the students’ goals (requirement 1), students’ feeling of autonomy (requirement 2), competence (requirement 3) and relatedness (requirement 4) within the game. According to SDT, if these four requirements are met, then the game should foster autonomous motivation’ (Proulx et al., 2016, p. 90).

The LM-GM-SDT mapping (Figure 4.4) has been formed to help identify and analyse game mechanics (driving game experience) and learning mechanics (driving learning activity) with the potential to support learners’ motivation (Proulx et al., 2016). Proulx et al. (2016) identify the mechanics that seem to have a clearer link with the SDT components of the self-determination continuum, which ranges from amotivation, through extrinsic motivation, to intrinsic motivation as its highest level. The mechanics for learning and games need to be analysed in the context they are used. For instance, a game mechanic can be used to scaffold an activity that can make the learning more engaging. However, if this activity is overly scaffolded through the instructional support provided by the teacher, the learners’ perception of autonomy may be reduced (Proulx et al., 2016). With progression, for instance, if too directed and hand-held the sense of autonomy will be lost, and learners will be overly dependent on the teacher’s validation and instructions. The level of external and intrinsic motivation provided by applied games has to be balanced to avoid under- and over-regulation situations (Romero and Lam-bropoulous, 2011), which could negatively impact the motivation of the learners (Proulx et al., 2016).

LM-GM has already demonstrated how game and learning mechanics can be mapped against very specific thinking skills towards designing and producing an effective learning tool. Proulx et al. (2016) suggest that the same principle can also apply to motivation by exploiting these mechanics in a way that fosters SDT components. This can help form our understanding on how to strategise the integration of the different mechanics in our design towards defining the triggers for learning that happen through gameplay. The design should take into consideration the way they may affect the SDT continuum perception of the learner, which links to the need to meet the motivational needs of our learners and also their ability. The strategy could also include phasing relevant triggers based on a player’s journey.
